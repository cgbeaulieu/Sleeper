The format

Our scoring has an unusual rule

This is a superflex league, so everyone starts two quarterbacks every week. The scoring also has a wrinkle that matters more than it looks:

Rushing and receiving first downs are worth half a point each. Passing first downs are worth nothing. A one-yard quarterback sneak pays the quarterback 0.6. A ten-yard completion on third down pays him 0.4, and pays his receiver 2.0.

Here is the same afternoon run through the league's actual settings:

280 pass yds, 2 TD, 1 INT, plus 45 rush yds, 1 rush TD, 4 rushing first downs
30.7
320 pass yds, 3 TD, 0 INT, no rushing stats
24.8

The penalty lands on the quarterback and nobody else. Receivers and backs get paid for moving the chains the same way runners do, so every rule in the book quietly pushes value away from the position we are all required to start two of.

Joe Burrow went seventeenth, which this publication considers the single largest error of the draft and will continue to consider so regardless of what happens over the next fourteen weeks.

The correction

What the first-down rule actually does

The draft-night read, including in an earlier version of this article, was that the rule creates two tiers of quarterback and that the runners are worth a lot more. So we measured it. Every 2025 season was scored through our settings, then scored again with the first-down rule switched off, and the two boards compared.

Among the top twenty quarterbacks, the rule is worth between zero points (Matthew Stafford, who had no rushing first downs at all) and seventeen points across a whole season (Drake Maye). That is a spread of one point per week. Not one quarterback moves even three places in the rankings when you turn the rule off.

It does bite somewhere. It bites at receiver.

So the rule everyone spent draft night talking about as a quarterback rule is a receiver rule that also happens to tax quarterbacks. Nobody drafted for it. Receiver picks tracked national rankings almost exactly, and national rankings are built for a scoring system that does not have this rule in it.

Where the value is

Quarterback is the third most valuable position here

The cleanest way to price a position is to ask what the best player at it is worth compared to the last guy who actually starts. In a ten-team superflex that means the eighteenth quarterback, the thirtieth back, the thirtieth receiver and the tenth tight end.

Six quarterbacks who threw at least 250 passes last season went undrafted and are sitting on waivers right now: Tua Tagovailoa, Michael Penix, Kirk Cousins, Joe Flacco, Mac Jones and Spencer Rattler. The position was never scarce. We just behaved as though it was.

A quarter of the first sixty picks went to quarterbacks. In a one-quarterback league that number is usually ten to thirteen percent. Superflex changed how we drafted. The scoring should have changed it back and did not.

The board

First QB selected, by team

Drafting is not the same as being wrong, and one season is not proof. But the only time this league has played superflex before, in 2025, the three managers who took a quarterback inside the first six picks finished fourth, fifth and sixth, and the podium went to the three who waited longest. That history gets its own piece.

Who Dey and the Blowfish

Paid the most

Josh Allen at 4 and Joe Burrow at 17. While everyone else was taking Puka Nacua and CeeDee Lamb your commissioner was buying arms, and the position he bought them at turns out to be the third most valuable one on the board. Writing the article that says this was a mistake is its own kind of penance. If the format rewards elite quarterbacks the way it was supposed to, this wins the league. If it does not, it is a very expensive pair of Bengals.

Rancor Fajitas

Mahomes at 52

Justin Herbert at 32 and Patrick Mahomes at 52, which is good value in a format that puts a ceiling on what a pure passer does for you. He also lost Matthew Stafford to Skinned Knee Varsity four picks before his turn and posted about it in the league chat while the draft was still running, which is the most relatable thing that happened all night.

Skinned Knee Varsity

Nine receivers

Nine wide receivers and two quarterbacks, which read as reckless on draft night and reads differently now. Receiver is where our first-down rule actually pays and he has more of them than anyone. The risk is real, because Stafford and Daniel Jones have to play every week with no cover for a bye or a hamstring, but he is the only manager who built something the rest of us did not.

4th and 35

Gibbs at 1.1

The newcomer drew the first overall pick and took Jahmyr Gibbs, which is the single most valuable asset in this scoring, since running back is the position with the biggest edge and Gibbs racks up first downs on the ground and through the air. Jayden Daniels at 21 is a fine second act. Better position than the team name suggests.

Puka Nacua and CeeDee Lamb to start, then Caleb Williams, Brock Purdy, Cam Ward and Aaron Rodgers. Four quarterbacks in a league where six NFL starters went undrafted is four more than the waiver wire required.

What actually decides this season

Three things worth knowing now

Receiver

The edge is sitting there unclaimed

Forty receivers move three or more places when the first-down rule is applied, with season swings up to thirty-seven points. Every ranking any of us read is built for standard scoring, so the chain-moving slot receiver is cheap in this league and will stay cheap until somebody starts buying. Ricky Pearsall type over Tory Horton type, all year, on waivers.

Schedule

The median matchup is not a parity rule

Playing the league median every week removes luck, and luck is what keeps weak teams alive. Simulating twenty thousand seasons with this league's own scoring variance, the median lifts the chance that the standings reflect the actual best teams from 74 to 77 percent, and cuts the worst team's playoff odds from 15 to 11 percent. Six of ten making the playoffs is what keeps this league open, and it is doing far more work than the median is.

Backfield

Running back is the scarce position

The best back is worth 15.1 points a week over a replacement starter, against 9.1 for a quarterback. Two managers spent premium capital on arms and one spent the first overall pick on Jahmyr Gibbs. The scoreboard will sort out which of those was the luxury.

Thirty quarterbacks were drafted for twenty starting spots and six NFL starters were left on the wire. Week 1 is the first evidence of whether that was a misread of the format or a correct read of it.
